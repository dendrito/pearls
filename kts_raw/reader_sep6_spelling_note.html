A note on the spelling of Hebrew words

Hebrew words here are spelled the way Rabbi Gaguine and his own congregation spelled them — which is not the way you will see them spelled almost anywhere else today. You will read Yom Tob rather than Yom Tov, Shabbath rather than Shabbat, Sepher rather than Sefer. These are not misprints. The Spanish and Portuguese Jews of London, Amsterdam and Livorno came to English by way of Spain and Portugal, and wrote Hebrew in Iberian spelling rather than the German-influenced spelling that produced the forms most readers know now. The author’s own name shows it: he signed himself Shem Tob Gaguine.

The spellings follow the London prayer book of de Sola and Gaster, revised under Ḥachám Solomon Gaon — the book he would have had in front of him. Four habits are worth knowing before you begin: b where you might expect v (Tob, Lulab, Ereb); th at the end of many words (Shabbath, Succóth, Abóth); ph where you might expect f (Sepher, Tephilah); and ng for the letter ayin, which these communities pronounced as a nasal (Shemang, Ta-ngamim).

Hard kaf divides on the vowel that follows it — c before a, o and u (Cohen, Ḥanuccáh, Succóth, Ashcabah), and k before e and i (Keter, Ketubah, Malkenu). It is the spelling of Spanish and Portuguese, where this rite’s books were printed.

Two letters carry a dot so the Hebrew can be recovered from the English. The prayer book writes ḥet and khaf alike as a plain h, and qof and hard kaf alike as a plain k; here ḥet is ḥ and qof is ḳ. The ḳ follows the romanization libraries use to catalogue Hebrew books, where every consonant maps to one letter. Without it Ḳidush (קידוש) and Kipur (כפור) would begin alike.

For ḥet the prayer book itself has no settled practice. Sometimes it prints a doubled hh, sometimes a plain h, and the choice follows neither the position of the letter nor any rule we could find — the same volume gives all four of these:

The same Hebrew letter, four spellings in one volume. We use one. The other throat letter, khaf, is left exactly as the book prints it — ch, as in Zachór and Hechál.

The acute marks the stressed syllable throughout — Malchutó, Sépher, Minḥáh, Shaḥaríth — and a stressed i is simply í: Sepharadím, ’Arbíth. A doubled consonant marks a dagesh in the Hebrew and shortens the vowel before it, as English does in later against latter: Ḳaddéesh, Ḳiddúsh, Ḥanuccáh, Tephilléen.

Five words keep an older London spelling, and they are the whole list. Each is printed that way in the prayer book, and each is among the words a reader meets most often; the ée is an older way of writing the same long i the rule now writes í, not a different sound.

Words in italics follow different rules. Tractates, books and rabbis — Moed Katan, Sefer HaManhig, Shulchan Aruch, Akiva — keep their ordinary scholarly spelling, because every reference here is meant to be looked up and a reference can only be followed if it is spelled the way the libraries spell it. The prose follows the rules above; the references follow the convention of the shelf; the italics tell you which you are reading.

The text is still being converted. These rules were settled in September 2026 and the translated pages now follow them. Pages still to be translated will arrive already in this spelling; if you meet an older form — Berahah for Berachá, Kadish for Ḳaddéesh — it is the same word.
